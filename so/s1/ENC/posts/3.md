Software engineers carefully architect and design their projects to ensure indented functionality without significant error.
A software engineer must navigate a nuanced ecosystem ensuring that they have accounted for the complexities of both their digital environment and the physical realities.

The document which will be analyzed in the rhetorical analysis is the RFC, know as a 'Request for Comments'.
The RFC is a proposal utilized by open source communities, large organizations, and standards bodies to formalize significant architecture decisions within a project.
The proposal presents an architectural decision, providing the necessary background required to results of the change if it is implemented.
They detail the exigence which has required the implementation of a new system with the project, explaining how the current state of the project must be modified in order to overcome a problem being encountered.
As part of the open nature of the document, issues with the proposal are addressed and unresolved issues are raised for further discussion, or _comments_.
The RFC is open by design, with the title of the genre literally inviting the audience to begin a discourse about the decision planned within.

# Response

Within the domain of software engineering, technical communication is not an isolated administrative task, but a consequential architecture that directly governs user behavior and digital autonomy. My values and beliefs originate from professional computing discourse communities—most notably the ACM and IEEE Codes of Ethics—paired with an enduring personal commitment to systems transparency and accountability. As J. Blake Scott explains, ethics represents the "relational, situational, power-laden negotiation of principles derived from various value systems with the goal of arriving at the best course of action" (218). Rather than treating ethics as an abstract or static code, I approach technical communication as an ongoing inquiry where authors must actively deliberate their obligations across complex networks of stakeholders.

A critical scenario in my field where ethical principles clash with organizational incentives involves the implementation of _dark patterns_. Developers and technical writers are frequently pressured by corporate management to deploy deceptive interface copy, confusing subscription cancellations, or intrusive telemetry pipelines designed to extract private user data without genuine consent. In these instances, an organization's pursuit of immediate financial metrics directly conflicts with the user's fundamental right to informed consent. Scott emphasizes that technical communication is _transsituational_ and _internetworked_, operating across interconnected contexts where communicators bear distinct "obligations to and consequences for users or audiences" (227). Furthermore, Scott urges communicators to examine how their documents respect "users' privacy and right to informed consent" and whether information is communicated in an accessible manner (227). When researching and authoring user-facing documentation, I resist coercive designs by utilizing Scott's heuristic of guiding questions. This entails auditing how consent flows are interpreted across differing user environments, refusing to conceal data harvesting behind deliberately obfuscated language, and advocating for transparent system defaults.

To sustain this ethical stance, I rely on professional ethical codes and Scott's conceptualization of _phronesis_, defined as the "virtue of practical thought" (210). Scott's heuristic provides an invaluable resource for mapping competing pressures, helping communicators balance fiduciary duties to an employer against ethical obligations to users and the broader public (226–28). By evaluating how power is distributed across a system, I can cultivate the practical wisdom necessary to negotiate organizational tensions and ensure my work promotes user welfare.
